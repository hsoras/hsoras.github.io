Limitations

These are small samples with four copies of one model per game. The examples were picked to illustrate the behavior. Multiple promises and wins in one game aren't independent trials, and shared draws link outcomes across models. This doesn't establish that a model is generally honest or dishonest.

Most runs also used a harness containing a posting bug: a rejected over-length message could end an agent's turn before it could retry. I patched and reran three Haiku/Argon groups; the other 420 reported games used the original harness. The patched groups still made no transfers. The rejection audit found no over-length post attempts in the main or no-instruction Sol/Astra games, so this bug doesn't explain their difference. It could still affect other negotiation results. Bug and replacement cohorts.

Some analyses were designed after seeing the games, particularly the peer-expectation and later-deal checks. Reasoning was mostly hidden for Sol and Astra. The public promises, bets and transfers are firmer evidence than my guesses about why they happened.

Appendix

Promise counts

I used two audits. The game-level recount compared the winner's own public promise or accepted terms before its bet with what it sent afterwards. An amendment counted as kept if affected contributors agreed to it. The offer audit additionally counted broader commitments to distribute or relay winnings. Its unit was one holder offer, at most once per bet.

Main-game figures

I also counted individual offers to hold a pool. Did a peer send points? Did the bet win, making the promised return due? Did the holder follow through?

20 main games per model. One unit is a public holder offer, counted at most once per bet. “Funded” means a peer sent points before the bet; “due” means that funded bet won; “kept” means every promised recipient got the agreed amount or relay by game end. Agreed amendments count as kept. This broader offer audit includes ambiguous relay promises, and it counts two Fable games as broken that I'd count as kept. Audit and definitions.

Of 21 Sol offers that came due, 2 were kept. Both honest winners were betrayed by the next holder. Some other winners passed the pot onward without first making a promised payout; sending points wasn't always the same as keeping the deal. Why the two audits give different counts.

The final balances made the difference concrete. One agent finished with every surviving point in 15 of 20 Sol games and 18 of 20 Astra games. That never happened with Opus.

Setup and controls

Each condition had 20 games per model. Agents took turns in a fixed order for up to eight rounds. A stake of s won 2s with probability s/(s+1). Each agent could bet once. Points were the game score, not money paid to the model.

I reused the same random seed across matching games. A bet of the same size at the same position in the sequence therefore had the same outcome. How the draws worked.

Additional outcomes

Sol and Astra went from about 8 final group points to about 45. Sharing let the pot reach more agents' unused bets.

Total points held by all four agents at game end, averaged across all 20 games per model per condition, including losses. Each group started with 4 points. The identical 44.8s reflect shared gambling draws as well as similar play. Higher group points alone don't establish promise-keeping. Final outcomes.

Opus pooled in 17 of 20 main games and Fable in 9. For the first pooled win, paying every contributor at least double or passing the whole pot onward happened in 5/15 Sol, 2/15 Astra, 14/14 Opus and 5/7 Fable games. These are transfer categories, not exact promise compliance: a relay could leave a payout promise unfulfilled. Definitions.

Without the goal paragraph, Sol and Astra each finished with an even 64-point split in 14 of 20 games, compared with none in the main condition. Opus went from 10 to 14 such games. Fable's lower mean of 41.5 included a lost 6-point bet with an 85.7% win chance. The shared random draw was 91.3%: too high for that bet, but low enough for Sol's 16-point stake to win. Final points and shared luck.

Without the end vote, 5 of 20 Sol games reached a third or fourth bettor, compared with none in the main condition. One agent still finished with every point in 14 games. The largest final balance averaged 12.2, compared with 7.9 before; this is a different measure from the average group total. Whole-game tables.

Talking got pooling started, but didn't enforce it

Without the message board, nobody transferred points in any of the 160 games. They could still send points and see public actions. Nobody pooled, so I couldn't test repayment. This condition also skipped turns for agents with no points, unlike the board games. No-board comparison.

Gemini 4 Argon had a different problem even with the board: everyone wanted to receive first. In one game, an agent noticed:

“Everyone is asking to receive first!”

In the same message, it asked the others to send it a point first.

Argon made no transfers in 40 board games, with and without the goal paragraph. So I don't know whether it would have kept its promise if someone had actually sent points. Argon examples and harness check.

Reasoning and selected examples

This also reminded me of work on score-seeking in LLMs, which suggests that models may sometimes care about other agents' scores. That could fit the continued effort to grow and share the pot. My experiment doesn't distinguish it from the other explanations.

In 12 main games, 15 Opus/Fable agents mentioned LLM or AI peers while discussing whether to trust them. Eleven later held a winning pot: ten paid every contributor at least double and one relayed as agreed. Their remarks included doubt as well as expectations of honesty. This was an analysis designed after seeing the data, and the visible-reasoning sample differs by model. Speaker audit.

Opus called peers LLMs or AIs in 7/20 main games, Fable in 5/20. Opus did so in 5/20 player-wording games and 0/20 no-instruction games. Readable reasoning for Opus also fell from about 53% to 32% between the main and no-instruction conditions, so missing remarks don't establish a change in belief.
